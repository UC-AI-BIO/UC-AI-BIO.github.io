          The AI Bio Lab develops artificial intelligence that is designed not only to perform, but
          also to be understood, tested, bounded, and supported by credible assurance evidence before
          it is entrusted with consequential decisions.
        </blockquote>

        The lab is led by Prof. Kelly Cohen in the University of Cincinnati College of Engineering and
        Applied Science. Its research brings together fuzzy logic, evolutionary learning, explainable
        AI, autonomy, biomedical intelligence, and safety assurance. The aim is practical: help close
        the gap between an impressive research demonstration and an AI-enabled system that can be
        responsibly deployed in the real world.

      title: 'Our Research Purpose'
      text: |
        AI is advancing rapidly, but high average accuracy is not enough in aviation, medicine,
        defense, transportation, finance, or other settings where failure can harm people or
        missions. These systems require evidence about how they behave, why they make decisions,
        where their limits lie, and what happens when conditions depart from the expected operating
        envelope.

        The AI Bio Lab addresses this assurance gap. We study AI architectures and engineering
        methods that can support transparency, traceability, robustness, verification, runtime
        protection, and human oversight. Fuzzy systems are a central part of this work because they
        can represent expert knowledge in linguistic rules, model uncertainty explicitly, and execute
        through mathematically defined inference that mimics human reasoning. They are not treated as
        a universal replacement for other methods; they are developed as one especially promising
        component within broader assurance-oriented and hybrid system architectures.

  - block: markdown
    content:
      title: 'Assurance Engineering Comes First'
      text: |
        The lab does not equate explainability with certification. A readable rule base can make an
        assurance case easier to construct, but readability alone does not prove that a system is
        safe. Credible deployment requires a broader engineering framework that may include:

        - clear requirements and traceability from intended behavior to implementation and test evidence;
        - explicit operating domains, assumptions, constraints, and known limitations;
        - formal contracts, invariants, stability arguments, or other mathematically grounded evidence where appropriate;
        - runtime monitors and independent safety mechanisms that can detect or prevent unsafe behavior;
        - robustness, uncertainty, edge-case, and out-of-distribution evaluation;
        - human authority, override mechanisms, fallback behavior, and attention to human factors;
        - data, model, and configuration control throughout the system life cycle;
        - structured safety or assurance cases that connect claims to evidence.

        <blockquote class="research-position">
          <strong>Our position:</strong> assured, explainable, and certifiable AI will become
          increasingly important, and fuzzy systems are among the architectures best positioned to
          contribute — especially when they are integrated into rigorous, system-level assurance
          strategies.
        </blockquote>

  - block: markdown
    content:
      title: 'Why Fuzzy and Genetic Fuzzy Systems Matter'
      text: |
        Fuzzy logic is attractive for safety- and mission-relevant AI because it offers a combination
        that is difficult to obtain in a single architecture: explicit representation of uncertainty,
        deterministic inference, compatibility with expert knowledge, and rules that can often be
        examined in human terms.

        #### Potential advantages

        - Linguistic rules can make reasoning pathways easier for domain experts to inspect and discuss.
        - Membership functions and inference mechanisms are explicit rather than hidden inside a large opaque model.
        - Outputs can be designed with known bounds and tested systematically across the input space.
        - Fuzzy approaches can represent uncertainty in measurements and in the meaning of linguistic concepts.
        - Genetic fuzzy systems can learn candidate rules and model structures from data while preserving an interpretable form.

        #### Research challenges we take seriously

        - Large rule bases can become difficult to interpret and verify.
        - Learned rules may be redundant, unstable, or semantically weak unless the learning process is constrained.
        - Membership functions and linguistic labels require careful validation with domain experts.
        - Deterministic behavior and mathematical bounds are valuable, but they do not by themselves establish safety or regulatory acceptance.
        - No single AI paradigm is sufficient for every subsystem; perception, estimation, optimization, control, and decision support may require different methods.

        The AI Bio Lab seeks impact through more than publications. Its work is intended to
        contribute to deployable methods, credible evidence, trained people, and long-term
        partnerships. The lab collaborates across academia, government, and industry and has applied
        fuzzy and intelligent systems to aerospace, autonomy, biomedical analysis, transportation,
        and other consequential problems.

        A particular strength is workforce development. Students learn to connect algorithms with
        domain physics, human judgment, safety constraints, and the realities of implementation.
        This prepares them to contribute in environments where technical performance must be matched
        by accountability and engineering discipline.
